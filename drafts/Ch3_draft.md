# Chapter Three — The Rite

*Draft v1 — first-person present, Adira's POV. New voice: free-spirited, wry, Violet-minus-modern; lyricism saved for the sending. Placeholder: "Sera." Guard-captain deliberately unnamed. Ronan's festering gash corrected here per outline.*

---

I know the sea has decided to give something back before I even open my eyes.

It's the light. Dawn here comes in wrong — grey and low and flat, no pink, no gold, none of the clean salt-shine of a Solara morning. The water sounds wrong too, hushing against the sand in a rhythm I don't recognize, like it's trying to remember a song it once knew and can't quite get right.

Or maybe that's just me.

I'm on my feet before Ronan can call my name, because I don't want him to have to. He's been on watch all night, sitting at the cave mouth with his back to the rock and his bad arm held careful against his ribs, and the set of his jaw this morning tells me everything I need to know before I even look down the beach.

"He's come in," Ronan says. Not a question. He doesn't look at me when he says it. "Dawn tide put him on the sand, about a hundred paces north."

*Him.* Not *a body*. Ronan has been counting the dead too.

Sera is still asleep, curled small in the cave with my ruined overshirt wrapped around her, and I'm glad of it. I'm glad she doesn't have to be awake for this part. There's no good way to wake a person and tell them the sea brought back the only one of the five it wanted to keep.

I follow Ronan down to the waterline.

The sand is cold under my feet, and wrong — pale grey, heavy, the grains too coarse and too even, nothing like the warm black shingle of home. I file that away without meaning to, the way I file weather. Wrong sand. Wrong light. Wrong tide.

The wrongness is piling up so fast I'm starting to lose the thread of it.

And then we reach him, and everything else stops mattering, because it's the captain.

---

He's lying on his side, one arm thrown out like he fell mid-stride and the sea finished the job. The tide has left him above the foam line, arranged almost carefully, which is the cruelest trick of it — as if the water wanted him found, wanted him *seen*.

I know him. Of course I know him. He taught me the slip-knot when I was nine, the one I still tie without thinking, and he used to say I'd learn the sea faster if I stopped arguing with it, and I never did stop arguing, and he'd laugh that big rolling laugh and call me a squall with legs.

I look at him now and none of that fits. The laugh is gone. The him is gone. What's left is a shell, heavy and wrong-colored, and I do not know how to hold the fact that the sea — *my* sea, the one that's carried me and kept me and sung to me my whole life — did this to one of my father's men.

That's the thing about your first real loss. You don't get to be good at it. You don't get the years of practice that make it a known shape. It's just new, and it doesn't fit anywhere, and you stand there in the grey light on a beach that isn't yours and you think, *oh. So this is what it costs.*

Ronan crouches beside the captain, and for a long moment neither of us says anything. Then Ronan reaches out, very gently, and closes the captain's eyes with two fingers, and he does it with a tenderness I have never seen him spend on anything but a sail in a storm.

"The sea only gives back what it's finished with," he says quietly. It's an old island saying. I've heard it a hundred times at a hundred sendings. It's different when it's one of ours.

"We should move him above the tide," I say, and my voice comes out steady, which surprises me, which I'm grateful for. "Out of the reach. He deserves better than the foam."

Ronan looks at me then, really looks, and something in his face eases. "There she is," he says. "The squall with legs."

It almost makes me smile. Almost.

---

We carry him up the beach together.

He's heavier than he looks — the sea has gotten into everything, into his wool and his boots and the slack weight of him — and we stagger with it, the three-legged stumble of two people trying to be gentle with something that can't feel gentleness anymore. I take his shoulders; Ronan takes his feet. Neither of us says it, but we're both pretending this is something other than what it is.

Sera finds us before we reach the rocks. I don't know how she knew. Maybe she heard us, or maybe she just felt the morning turn. She comes down the beach with my overshirt still around her shoulders, and when she sees who we're carrying she stops — just for a breath, just long enough to take it in — and then her chin comes up, and she doesn't look away.

That's the thing about Sera. She doesn't flinch from the hard things; she walks at them.

She comes the rest of the way to us, and she squares her shoulders — and I have never loved her more than I do in that moment — and she takes a corner of the captain's sleeve, and she holds it, and she carries her part of him up the beach without a single word of complaint, because of course she does.

The four of us make it to the rock shelter together. The three of us, and him.

---

The rest of the day is the long kind.

We have no sea-silk. We have no obsidian sinkers. We have no deep abyssal channel to give him to, because whatever channel runs off this shore, I can't find it, and I have lain flat and listened three times and every time the water has told me the same wrong thing: *there is no deep here. There is no path out. There is only this.*

So we make do, because islanders always make do, and because the alternative is leaving him on the sand for the gulls, and I will not do that. I will not.

We have torn sailcloth, so that becomes his shroud instead of white sea-silk, and it is the wrong color — a faded grey-brown, not the clean white of a proper sending — but it is what we have. We have stones, the heavy wrong-grey stones of this beach, and those become his sinkers instead of obsidian, and they are the wrong weight, and I hate them. I hate every single one of them as I carry them up from the waterline, because they are not obsidian, because they will not take him home, because this whole shore is a bad imitation of the world and we are burying a good man in it.

Ronan catches me glaring at a stone and says, "It's not the rock's fault, Adira."

"I know whose fault it is," I say.

And there it is. The thing I've been keeping at the back of my teeth all day. It slips out sharp and quiet, and Ronan goes still beside me, and I know he heard it, and I know he won't let it pass.

"Whose?" he asks, and his voice is very careful.

*Mine,* I don't say. *I was at the helm. I felt the current go missing and I called it too late, and five men went into the black because I didn't trust what my own spine was telling me.*

But we are standing over the captain's body, and this is not the moment for my guilt to have a voice, so I shake my head and go back to the stones, and I let the work take the rest of it.

It doesn't take it. But it holds it down, which will have to do.

---

The sun goes down the way it came up — grey and flat and indifferent — and we carry the captain down to the water one last time.

This is the rite. I have spoken it a hundred times for other people's dead. I know every stanza, every pause, the exact place where the sea is meant to take the offering and the exact silence where the grief is supposed to go. I have never once spoken it and meant it like this.

We stand at the waterline, the three of us, with the sailcloth-wrapped weight of him between us, and the wrong tide licking at our ankles, and I open my mouth and the sending stanzas come out of me in the old tongue, the one my mother gave me, the one the sea itself was sung into.

The words are right. The water is wrong.

That's the whole of it, I think, as the last stanza leaves me. The words have been right my entire life. And the water — the water has stopped being right, and I am the only one who can feel the difference, and I am standing knee-deep in it with a dead man in my arms.

Sera sings the mourning stanza next. And her voice does not break. Not once. It comes out clear and steady and almost too strong for this grey shore — a woman's voice, not a girl's — and she sings it through, the way she has done everything since we woke on the sand, and it is the steadiness of it, more than any grief, that finally cracks something open in my chest.

Ronan doesn't take his eyes off her the whole time she sings. I don't think he knows he's doing it. I don't think he'd want me to know I saw.

Ronan carries him out. That's the last part. It's always the strongest who carries them out, and Ronan is the strongest, and so Ronan wades into the wrong grey water up to his waist with the captain in his arms, and he lets him go, and the sea takes him.

Not gently. It doesn't take him gently at all. It takes him the way it takes everything here — with the wrong tide, into the wrong current, toward no channel I can feel.

And I watch, and I do not cry, because if I start I will not stop, and someone has to keep standing.

When Ronan comes back out of the water, he's soaked and shivering and grey with the cold of it, and he walks straight to me, and he puts his cold wet hand on the back of my neck, and he says, very quietly, "He's with the deep now."

It's what you say. It's the response. It means *the sea has him, and the sea was always going to have us all, and that's not a tragedy, that's just the way of it.*

Only it isn't, here. Here, the deep isn't the deep. Here, there is no deep. Here, the water that took him is the same wrong water that's going to hold us all, and I have never in my life been more afraid of the one thing I've always loved.

---

We walk back up the beach in the dark.

Sera is quiet. Ronan is quiet, and moving slower than he was this morning, favoring his right side, and when I glance at him I catch him pressing his hand to his ribs and pulling it away the second he sees me look.

"Let me see it," I say.

"See what?"

"Ronan."

He sighs, the put-upon sigh of a man who knows he's about to lose, and stops, and lifts the edge of his torn shirt.

It's worse than he told me. A gash across the ribs, long and ugly and wrong-colored at the edges, crusted with salt and old blood and something that might be the beginning of infection. He'd said he twisted something getting off the wreck. He'd lied, or been kind, or both, and I've been too wrapped in my own wrongness to catch it.

"Ronan, that needs —" I stop, because I don't know what it needs. A healer. Clean water. A fire to boil the water in. We have none of it. We have a soaked fire-striker and one cask of water we're already rationing and a beach full of stones I hate.

"It's nothing," he says, dropping the shirt. "A scratch."

"Your scratches are usually sea-urchin spines and coral rot," I say. "This is —"

"This is a scratch," he says again, and there's the edge of a grin in it, the old Ronan surfacing, "that I will *allow* you to fuss over tomorrow, when there's light. Tonight, I want to sleep, and I want Sera to sleep, and I want you to sleep, and I want the sea to shut up for one single night."

"The sea doesn't shut up," I say.

"I know," he says. "That's the problem."

And then Sera, quietly, from behind us: "Neither do you two."

And for the first time all day — the first time since the sky went out — the sound that comes out of me is almost a laugh, and it surprises me so much that I nearly trip over my own feet in the dark.

Tomorrow, I think. Tomorrow I'll climb the headland, and the world will be the world again, and the Song will come back, and I'll know where I am, and I'll stop feeling the water going wrong under everything.

Tomorrow, I'll know.

Tonight, there are three of us, and a cold cave, and a dead man's memory, and a wound that needs more than we have.

It will have to be enough.

It's not enough.

---

*Later, I will think about this night and understand what it was: the first time I stood on the wrong shore and watched the sea I loved do something I couldn't read. It wouldn't be the last. But that night, walking back up the wrong sand in the wrong dark, I didn't know any of that yet. I only knew that the water was wrong, and that I was the only one who could feel it, and that I had never felt more alone with a truth in my entire life.*
