# Chapter Two — The Wake on the Sand

*Draft v2 — first-person present, Adira's POV. More dialogue; Sera shown as the steady, competent one; subtle Ronan→Sera hints (kept light). Locket check planted for Ch6.*

---

I wake with my mouth full of the sea, and the first coherent thought I have is that the world has stopped moving.

That's what does it. Not the salt, not the cold, not the thousand small agonies of being alive when I shouldn't be. It's the *stillness*. The ground doesn't sway. The ground doesn't breathe. I've spent my whole life on surfaces that moved under me, and this one is flat and dead and utterly indifferent, and the wrongness of it hauls me up out of the dark like a hook.

I roll over and retch seawater into the sand.

Then I hear him.

"Breathe. *Breathe.* Come on, Sera, you stubborn little — that's it. That's it. There she is."

Ronan's voice, hoarse and ragged, a man arguing with a god who isn't listening. I turn toward the sound and the world swings and rights itself, and I see him down at the waterline, on his knees in the foam, bent over a body, pressing and pressing and pressing.

Sera.

I'm up before I know I can stand. The sand drags at my feet — wrong sand, pale and coarse, nothing like the black shingle of home — and I run, and I fall once, and I get up, because if I don't get there I will never forgive myself, and because I am not losing another person today, not if I have to drag the whole sea back with my bare hands.

By the time I drop to my knees in the surf, Sera is already coughing up the ocean.

She does it the way she does everything — without any fuss. One violent convulsion, then she turns her head and gives the water back its due, and then she's trying to *sit up*, pushing at the sand with shaking arms, already reaching for the person next to her instead of the other way around.

"The others," she rasps. It's the first thing she says. Not *help me*, not *where am I*. "Where are the others? Ronan. *Where are the others?*"

Ronan doesn't answer. He just sits back into the surf, and I watch the tension go out of him all at once, and it's only then I realize he's been holding something together this whole time that I've never once seen him hold.

"You're going to want to lie back down," I tell Sera, and my voice sounds like someone else's, but it's steady, so I'll take it. "The world's going to spin."

"The world can spin later," she says, and she's already pushing herself upright, already looking past us, down the beach. Counting. She's *counting*. "There were five guards. Where are they?"

And that's Sera, I think, with something very like love. Half-drowned, freezing, and her first act on waking is to take inventory of the dead.

"Five," Ronan says, and it isn't his voice. "There were five. They're not —" He stops, and starts again. "The current ran north. We ran with it. If they're not here, we're not reaching them before dark."

There's a silence then, the three of us sitting in the cold surf, and the water running around our knees, indifferent.

It's Sera who stands first.

She sways, and catches herself, and shakes her head once like she's clearing water from her ears, and then she's up, and she's looking at the wreckage scattered along the tide line with the same flat, practical eye she used on the luggage.

"Right," she says. "Tide's coming. We need high ground, a cave if this shore's got one, and we need it before the light goes. Adira, can you walk? Ronan, can you carry?"

Ronan blinks at her. "Can I —"

"I'll take Adira," she says, and she's already got my arm, and her grip is stronger than it has any right to be. "You scout the high ground. You're the only one of us who can see in this gloom, and I'm not carrying both of you."

He looks at her for a long moment — and there's something in his face, quick and gone, that I don't have the words for — and then he grins, and it's the old grin, half real. "Yes, captain."

Sera snorts. "I outrank you. I've always outranked you."

And just like that, impossibly, the three of us are moving up the beach together, and it isn't hopeless, not quite, because Sera has given us something to do, and that's the thing about Sera: she always has something for your hands when your head's gone.

---

We find the cave the way you find anything on a strange shore — by luck, by darkness, and by the plain animal need to be out of the wind.

It's a gash in the rock above the tide line, low enough to duck into, deep enough to keep the spray off. The floor is stone and sand and old shell. There's no fire, no food, nothing dry in the whole of it. It smells of salt and bird and the cold green rot of things the sea abandoned.

Ronan sets me down inside — he's been half-carrying me the last hundred paces, whatever I said about walking — and then he straightens, and stops, one hand braced against his ribs, and the careful way he's been holding his right side all the way up the beach is suddenly, horribly visible.

"Ronan."

"I'm fine."

"You're holding your ribs like a man who's very much not fine," Sera says, and she's already crossing the cave toward him, already rolling up her sodden sleeves. "Let me see it."

"It's a scratch."

"Then let me see the scratch."

There's a brief, silent war — Ronan, who has never once admitted to being hurt in his life, against Sera, who once made a guard-captain sit still while she dug coral out of his palm. Sera wins. She always wins.

He lifts the shirt. The gash is long and ugly, crusted with salt and old blood, and the edges are already going wrong-colored.

Sera's face doesn't change. That's the thing about her — she could be looking at a splinter or a spear wound and you'd never know which from her face. She just reaches into the small pouch at her belt, the one she never goes anywhere without, and comes up with a wad of crushed leaves and a strip of sea-silk.

"This will sting," she says, not as a warning, as a fact. "And it's going to need more than I've got if it turns. So no heroics. No 'it's just a scratch.' You tell me the second it burns, do you hear me?"

Ronan mutters something that might be *yes* or might be *women*.

Sera presses the leaves to the wound, and Ronan's whole body goes rigid, and through it he says, absolutely evenly, "That's very soothing, thank you, Sera."

She smiles, small and private, and doesn't look up from her work. "I know."

And I watch the two of them, and I file the look on his face away without knowing why — the way he's holding very still, the way his eyes keep going to the top of her head instead of anywhere near the wound — and I think, distantly, that I'm glad she's here. That we're all here. That the sea, for all its cruelty, gave me back the two people I can't do any of this without.

---

Later, when Sera has finally slept and Ronan has taken the watch, I sit in the cave mouth with my back to the wrong stone, and I let my hand find my throat.

The locket is still there. Cool and whole, the cord unbroken, the obsidian smooth against my palm. The one thing in the world the sea gave back.

I don't know if it's relief or guilt that floods through me. I'm alive, and it's alive, and five men are not, and I don't get to sort any of that out tonight. So I close my fingers around it until it warms, and I hold on.

Tomorrow, I think. Tomorrow I'll climb the headland, and I'll look, and the world will be the world again.

---

The dark is absolute.

I know the dark of the sea. Even on the blackest night there's always something — stars, the phosphor burning in the wake, the slow silver of the swell under the moon. The sea is never truly dark. It holds light the way the palms hold rain, just under the skin.

This isn't the sea. This is the inside of a stone, and it's dark the way drowning is dark.

Ronan has taken the watch, or the watch has taken him — I can hear his breathing at the cave mouth, slow and careful, the rhythm of a man pretending the pain in his side is a thing he can outlast. Sera is asleep against my back, and her hands, even in sleep, are folded over the little pouch of leaves like she's ready to be needed again.

I should sleep. I can't.

So I do the thing I always do. I lie flat, spine against the stone, palms down, and I listen.

This is the oldest thing in me — older than the Song, older than my name. A wayfinder reads the sea through the body, through soles and spine, through the long slow language of pressure and temperature and swell. I've done it since I was old enough to lie still.

I don't have to think to know it.

The water is wrong.

Not wrong the way a storm is wrong, not wrong the way a current running against the season is wrong. Wrong the way a heartbeat is wrong when it stops being the one you know. The deep pulse that should be running under this shore — the cold river the Song names in every stanza of every crossing — is not there. In its place is something else, something long and slow and turning, that moves against the shore the way a thing moves when it has no intention of letting anything go.

I lie very still.

And I say nothing.

Because I'm the wayfinder, and they're looking to me, and the one thing I know for certain is that if I say it aloud — *this shore isn't in the Song* — they'll know, the way I now know, that we are somewhere no one has ever sung a stanza for.

Tomorrow, I'll climb the headland, and I'll look, and the Song will come back to me, and I'll stop feeling the water going wrong under everything.

Tomorrow, I'll know.

Tonight, I keep it in my chest, where it can't hurt them.

The water is wrong.

And I'm the only one who can feel it.
