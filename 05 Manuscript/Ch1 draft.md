# Chapter One — The Morning of Departure

*Draft v2 — first-person present, Adira's POV. Lighter pre-storm register; more dialogue; mainlanders reference removed; betrothal as accepted fact, not a death march.*

---

I wake before the gulls, which is either a good omen or a bad one depending on which of my father's sailors you ask, and I've asked them all, and they all disagree.

The morning is doing that thing Solara does when the season turns — pink at the edges, gold where the caldera smokes — and the sea is flat and bright and patient, like it's already waiting for us. I stand barefoot on the star terrace of my father's house and let the wind have my hair, and I feel, for the first time in weeks, something dangerously close to peace.

Four days to Korvath. Four days of open water and a clean deck under my feet. Whatever waits on the far shore, it can wait four more days.

"You're smiling." Ronan's voice, behind me, dry as old rope. "That's unsettling. Stop it."

I don't turn. "I'm smiling because I'm leaving you behind on the beach."

"Leaving *me* behind? I'm your escort, you absolute menace. I'm going *with* you." He drops onto the wall beside me, close enough to knock his shoulder into mine. "You're stuck with me across the whole Ring, Solari. Smile while you can. You'll be sick of me by Navira."

"Bold of you to assume I'm not sick of you already."

He grins — the quick, sun-cracked grin that has gotten him out of every beating he's ever started — and tips his head back to the sky. "Look at you. Four days out and the great wayfinder can't even read her own good luck."

"Good luck doesn't come in pink. Pink means the wind's holding, and the wind holding means the crossing's easy, and an easy crossing means —" I stop. "Actually, no. An easy crossing means four days without listening to you talk about your spear."

"My spear has feelings."

"Your spear is a stick with delusions."

We sit there a while, and it's good, the easy back-and-forth of it, the way it's been since we were children scraping our knees on the same black sand. Below us the harbor is waking up — oars clacking, voices carrying across the water, the big double-hulled voyager already riding at her mooring with the morning light in her sails.

My ship. *My* crossing. For all the talk of tradition and duty, there's this: the sea, four days of it, and a deck under my bare feet. I'd be lying if I said I wasn't a little hungry for it.

"Sera's already down there," Ronan says, and something in his voice changes the way it always does when her name comes up — a half-beat slower, like a man remembering to be careful. "Says you've packed half the house and forgotten your own spare boots."

"Then she's already packed my spare boots and lectured me about them twice."

"Three times. I heard her from the terrace."

I laugh, and push myself up off the wall. "Then I'd better go down and get my lecture before she gives it to the guards instead."

---

The farewell isn't a funeral. That's the thing nobody tells you about leaving — most of it is just *leaving*, with all its small, ordinary embarrassments.

My father waits on the shingle with half the household behind him, and he is doing the thing he does when he's trying not to hover, which is to inspect the ropes as if he'd never seen rope before. When I reach him he takes my face in his two hands, and he's smiling, and his eyes are doing the thing they do that means he's proud and worried in exactly equal measure and has no intention of saying either.

"Sing the compass well, daughter," he says.

"And trust the water that knows me," I finish, because it's the response, because I've been saying it since I could talk. "Papa, you said that twice at breakfast."

"I'm practicing. In case you've forgotten."

"I'm a Master Wayfinder."

"You're *my* Master Wayfinder." He presses his forehead to mine — the *te hā*, one shared breath — and when he pulls back he's grinning again, and he squeezes my shoulders once, hard, the way he did when I was small enough to fit under one arm. "Go. Before I start inspecting the *water*."

---

Sera has everything stowed before I'm even up the plank. Of course she does. She's standing at the stern locker with her hands on her hips, surveying the deck like a general, and the two youngest guards are already cringing under the weight of her stare.

"I packed your boots," she says, without looking at me. "And the spare cord. And the sea-silk for the cold nights, which you would have forgotten. And no, before you ask, I did not pack the good luck charm, because you don't have one, because you're not twelve."

"Good morning to you too."

"It was a good morning until someone left her belt on the terrace." She holds it up, one finger, like a magistrate presenting evidence. "Do you know how much trouble I went to to get you presentable for this voyage?"

"An alarming amount, I'm sure."

"*An alarming amount,*" she echoes, and there's the ghost of a smile on her, the one she hides when she's pretending to be severe. "Get on the ship, Adira."

I get on the ship. I'm smiling. I can't help it.

---

The shove-off is clean, because Ronan is strong and the guards know their work and Sera has, against all odds, remembered everything in the entire world and packed it into one hull.

The paddles bite. The shore of Solara falls away behind us in long green strokes, the star terraces shrinking to white lines on the cliff, the caldera leaning its smoke out over the water like it's waving us off. For a while we sail through gold, and the sea is a flat bright mirror, and the wind sits exactly where the Song says it should.

I take my place at the steering sweep, and it fits my hands like it was made for them, because it was, because I've steered this ship since I was tall enough to see over the rail.

And then I do the thing I always do, the thing I've done since I was a girl: I leave the sweep to Ronan, and I lie flat on the deck, my back against the wood, my bare soles against the hull, and I listen.

This is the part of me no betrothal gets to touch.

The sea speaks in pressure, not words — in the long slow breathing of the swells against the hull, in the temperature of the water under the wood, in the way the current curls and releases like a muscle. A wayfinder learns to read it the way other people read a face. I've been doing it so long it isn't a skill anymore. It's the shape my body makes when it's at rest.

The swell is clean. The current runs true. The wind sits in the third stanza, the one about the morning star and the gull that rides the shoulder of the wind.

*Perfect,* I think. *Four days of this.*

Which is, of course, the exact moment I feel it.

The deep current. The one that should be running beneath us like a slow cold river — the one the Song names in every crossing, the one I've felt under every voyage since I first lay on a hull as a girl. It isn't there. In its place there's something else, something long and turning, and it does not feel like any water I have ever known.

I sit up. I look at the sky.

Pink and gold. High, innocent clouds. No weather in any of them.

And out on the horizon, past the white line of the reef, the water has begun to change color.

"Ronan," I say.

He's there in three strides, because he knows that tone. "What?"

"Look at the sky and tell me what you see."

He looks. "Clear. Pink. No weather."

"Now the water."

He looks. And goes still.

The sea at the horizon is no longer a mirror. It's a bruise — dark, glossy, *wrong* — spreading toward us faster than any cloud, faster than any current has a right to move.

"The wind's turning," the guard-captain calls from the bow, and there's the first thread of fear in it.

No, I think. The wind never turned at all.

"Sails down," I hear myself say, and my voice is calm in a way my heart isn't. "Now. All of them. Sera, get below the —"

The sky goes out.

One moment it's pink and gold and innocent, and the next it's the color of a closed fist, and the wind hits like the flat of a god's hand, and the world tips, and the sea that raised me rises up and swallows the light.

I have been at sea since before I could walk.

And I have never once seen it look at me like that.

The mast screams. Ronan is shouting my name. The hull bucks like a living thing, and the Song — every stanza I've ever trusted — goes silent in my head, and there is only the black water, and the cold, and the dark.

And then, nothing at all.
